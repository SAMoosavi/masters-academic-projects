ABSTRACT
Vehicular Ad Hoc Networks (VANETs) are one of the essential and exciting types of ad
hoc networks in the present intelligent transportation systems where the group of vehicles
communicate to ensure vehicular safety and improve the standard of living for those who
drive. While in communication, VANETs are vulnerable to privacy and security attacks. To
provide authenticity in VANETs, signed messages are verified by road side units. If the number
of messages increases, it will increase the time to verify every message. To minimize the
computational overhead in VANETs and to verify each signature, it is enough to verify a single
signature called aggregate signature. This paper provides a critical survey on different ways
of computing pairing-free certificateless aggregate signature (CLAS) based on Elliptic Curve
Cryptography(ECC). A CLAS scheme is a digital signature that combines multiple signatures
from different messages coming from different vehicles into one single short signature to keep
the signature verification process at a low cost and to reduce the signature size. This paper
focuses on the thorough overview of ECC-based CLAS schemes, we also continue our work by
performing a rigorous security analysis of a recent quantum-secure CLAS scheme and exposing
a crucial weakness in it.

1. Introduction
Vehicular Ad-hoc Networks (VANETs) signify a groundbreaking advancement in Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS),
facilitating instantaneous communication between vehicles and roadside infrastructure. Utilizing Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) and
Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I) communication paradigms, VANETs enable the sharing of safety-critical information, traffic conditions, and environmental data, which significantly enhance road safety and traffic efficiency. The fundamental architecture of
VANETs, as shown in Fig. 1, comprises three primary entities: a Trusted Authority (TA) responsible for system initialization and
management, includes two components Tracing Authority (TRA) to trace back malicious vehicles and Key Generation Center (KGC)
to generate part of the keys to the vehicles. Road Side Units (RSUs) serving as communication intermediaries, and On-Board Units
(OBUs) embedded within vehicles for data collection and dissemination [1]. These components communicate via wireless protocols
such as IEEE 802.11p and cellular networks (3G/4G) [2,3], operating under the Dedicated Short-Range Communications (DSRC)
standard [4].
However, the open wireless nature and high mobility characteristics of VANETs introduce significant security and privacy
vulnerabilities. The network faces threats including message forgery, impersonation attacks, privacy breaches, and denial-of-service
attempts. Simultaneously, VANETs demand stringent requirements: messages must be authenticated to ensure integrity, yet vehicle
privacy must be preserved to prevent tracking. Moreover, traffic safety applications demand real-time responsiveness, requiring
ultra-low latency and high computational efficiency. Traditional Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) based solutions, while secure, impose
substantial certificate management overhead that is unsuitable for the dynamic VANETs environment. Identity-Based Cryptography
(IBC), suggested as a substitute, removes the need for certificates but faces the fundamental issue of key escrow, as KGC holds all
private keys. Certificateless Public Key Cryptography (CL-PKC) was introduced by Al-Riyami et al. [5] elegantly resolves both issues
by distributing key generation between the TA and users, eliminating both the certificate management burden and complete key
escrow. To address the computational bottleneck of verifying numerous messages from multiple vehicles, Boneh et al. [6] introduced
aggregate signatures, enabling a single signature that combines numerous signatures into a compact signature that is verifiable in

one operation. When combined with certificateless cryptography, Certificateless Aggregate Signature (CLAS) schemes offer an ideal

Among various mathematical foundations for CLAS schemes, Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC) has emerged as particularly
promising for resource-constrained vehicular environments. Compared to RSA and bilinear pairing-based approaches, ECC provides
comparable security levels while utilizing much smaller key sizes and reduced computational complexity. For example, a 256-bit
ECC key is as safe as a 3072-bit RSA key [7,8], resulting in faster signing and verification operations, lower bandwidth consumption,
and reduced storage requirements—critical factors for OBUs with limited processing power and memory [9].
Within last ten years many pairing-free ECC-based CLAS schemes have been suggested in terms of computational efficiency.
However most of these schemes subsequently found to be insecure by other researchers who demonstrated concrete attacks and
proposed improved schemes, creating recurring cycle of cryptanalysis and improvement. Despite this, no comprehensive survey
exists that consolidates these findings and systematically evaluates all existing pairing-free ECC-based CLAS schemes under a unified
security framework. This motivates our work to perform rigorous mathematical cryptanalysis on each scheme, find areas of weakness,
and provide a concise taxonomy of attacks to be use in the future. The key contributions of our paper as follows.
• Survey of pairing-based CLAS schemes, categorized according to the recurring security attacks they are susceptible to, such as
public key replacement attack, forgery attack, etc in the literature.
• Comprehensive description and evidence-based analysis of known attacks.
• New vulnerability (Cancellation Attack) discovered in a recent quantum-secure CLAS scheme [10].
• Significant contrast of ECC-based CLAS schemes (contributions, strengths, limitations).
• Full-fledged performance measurement in terms of computational complexity and security.

2. Aggregate signature fundamentals
In this section, we will discuss aggregate signatures and the different types of aggregate signatures in detail. An aggregate
signature is composed of four algorithms: Key-Gen, Sign, Combine, and Verify. The initial two are similar to the basic digital signature
concept. An 𝑛 tuple vector with triples serves as input to the algorithm Combine, which is composed of a message (𝑚𝑖 ), a signature
(𝜎𝑖 ), and a public key (𝑝𝑘𝑖 ). A single signature 𝜎𝑖 is produced by the Sign process, and it acts as a signature for each of the messages
that are provided. 𝜎 is referred to as an aggregate signature, and it should be the same length as signature on one message. Lastly,
a vector of 𝑛 pairs (𝑝𝑘𝑖 , 𝑚𝑖 ) and aggregate signature 𝜎 are given as input to the algorithm Verify. Finally, it will produce the output
valid, only if 𝜎 is the aggregate signature of the 𝑛 valid signatures; otherwise, the output is invalid.
Combine algorithm is used to combine numerous message signatures from various signers into a single, brief signature, as shown
in Fig. 2. Anyone can verify, using the aggregate signature, that each individual signer correctly signed their respective message
using their own public key. Anyone can do this aggregation, and no secret keys are needed. All the public keys and original messages
must be required to verify the aggregated signature [11].

Aggregate signature methods come in three varieties: multi-signatures, non-sequential signatures, and sequential aggregate
signatures. In multi-signature schemes, each participant 𝑈𝑖 contributes their signature 𝜎𝑖 to the same message 𝑀. All participants
end up endorsing the identical message. A collection of signatures 𝜎𝑖 and public keys 𝑝𝑘𝑖 can be aggregated by a third party to
produce the final signature (𝜎) ← Mulsig(𝜎𝑖 , 𝑝𝑘𝑖 , 𝑀) which is then transmitted to a verifier. Subsequently, the verifier employs 𝜎
along with the pairs (𝑝𝑘𝑖 , 𝑀) to assess the legitimacy of 𝜎 for 𝑖 signers utilizing 𝑝𝑘𝑖 and the message 𝑀.
In non-sequential aggregate signature schemes, 𝜎𝑖 represents the signature generated by each user 𝑈𝑖 as they sign a distinct
message 𝑀𝑖 with their private key 𝑠𝑘𝑖 . Any outside group, such as an aggregating party or a user, combines public keys with a set
of signatures 𝜎𝑖 to make the final unified signature 𝜎 ← Aggregate(𝜎𝑖 , 𝑝𝑘𝑖 , 𝑀𝑖 ). After that, a verifier uses 𝜎 and the pairs (𝑝𝑘𝑖 , 𝑀𝑖 )
to check if 𝜎 is legitimate or not. The non-sequential aggregate signature schemes are the independent combination of the group of
messages, signature and the public keys with reference to the sequence of the parties involved. In sequential aggregate signatures,
aggregation happens when each signer gets an aggregate-so-far signature and adds it to their own signature [12].

3.1. ECC
ECC has emerged as a robust cryptographic technique that offers safe data protection and transmission in a variety of fields. A
strong foundation for key exchange, digital signatures, and encryption is provided by ECC.
The fundamental concept of ECC lies in the mathematical properties of elliptic curves defined over finite fields. The mathematical
operations of ECC define the elliptic curve.

3.2. Computational hardness assumptions
ECC relies on several fundamental computational hardness assumptions to guarantee its security. These assumptions are the
mathematical foundation for ECC-based cryptographic algorithms and are outlined as follows.

• Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP): Security of ECC is based on the intractability of the discrete logarithm
on elliptic curves. A point on the curve, e.g. a point 𝑃 = 𝑘𝐺, where 𝐺 is a known point and 𝑘 is an unknown scalar, is
computationally infeasible to compute 𝑘 given (𝑃 , 𝐺). The inability to efficiently solve this problem provides the basis for
ECC’s cryptographic strength.
• Elliptic Curve Computational Diffie–Hellman Problem (ECCDH): Let 𝐸 is an elliptic curve over a finite field, and that G is a base
point of elliptic curve 𝐸. Two users select private keys 𝑎 and 𝑏, and compute their respective public keys as 𝑃 = 𝑎𝐺 and
𝑄 = 𝑏𝐺. ECCDH assumption states that, given (𝐺, 𝑃 , 𝑄), it is hard to compute the shared secret 𝑆 = 𝑎𝑏𝐺 [14].
• Elliptic Curve Decisional Diffie–Hellman Problem (ECDDH): Given an elliptic curve 𝐸, a generator point 𝐺, and three points
𝐴 = 𝑎𝐺, 𝐵 = 𝑏𝐺, and 𝐶 = 𝑐𝐺, determine whether 𝐶 = 𝑎𝑏𝐺. In other words, the task is to decide whether the tuple (𝐺, 𝐴, 𝐵, 𝐶)
represents a valid Diffie–Hellman tuple. This problem is computationally hard and closely related to the ECCDH.

3.4. Security model for CLAS in VANETs
Generally three categories of Adversary Models are defined for CLAS schemes, 𝑇 𝑦𝑝𝑒-𝐼 𝐴𝑑𝑣𝑒𝑟𝑠𝑎𝑟𝑦(1 ), 𝑇 𝑦𝑝𝑒-𝐼𝐼 𝐴𝑑𝑣𝑒𝑟𝑠𝑎𝑟𝑦(2 ),
and 𝑇 𝑦𝑝𝑒-𝐼𝐼𝐼 𝐴𝑑𝑣𝑒𝑟𝑠𝑎𝑟𝑦(3 ) based on the behavior of attackers.
1. Type-I Adversary(1 ): Models an external attacker who can replace public keys but does not have access to the master secret
key. Represents malicious users or outsiders.
2. Type-II Adversary(2 ): Models a malicious KGC who possesses the master secret key but cannot replace public keys. Represents
insider attacks.
3. Type-III Adversary(3 ): Represents a malicious insider capable of compromising 𝑛 vehicles to perform a collusion attack. By
obtaining both the partial private keys and full private keys of these vehicles, the adversary can forge 𝑛 signatures that pass
batch verification, even though some individual signatures are invalid.
A CLAS scheme is considered secure whenever it meets the following properties:
1. Correctness: For any legitimately generated signatures, the Verify algorithm always outputs 1.
2. Existential Unforgeability under Adaptive Chosen Message Attack (EUF-CMA): There is no polynomial time adversary that can
construct a valid aggregate signature of a set of messages without at least having a single private key of the associated identity.

3.5. Security requirements for CLAS scheme
To achieve security and privacy in VANETs, the following fundamental requirements must be established [15,16].
1. Message Authentication(1 ): The receiver must verify that the messages and signatures received by other vehicles are not
modified in the network.
2. Identity Privacy Preserving(2 ): Only TA Knows the true identity of a vehicle, which is kept under wraps.
3. Traceability(3 ): In the event of a malevolent vehicle, TA can disclose the true identity of the vehicle.
4. Unlinkability(4 ): Two or more messages cannot be linked to the same vehicle. That is the adversary should not be able to
link two or more communications originating from the same vehicle.
5. Non-Repudiation (5 ): No entity can deny the signature on the message later.
6. Resistance to Replay attack(6 ): By checking the Timestamp 𝑡𝑖 , the verifier can address the replay attacks.
7. Resistance to Impersonation attack(7 ): The network must be able to identify the attacker who created the signature for the
message, if any vehicle in VANETs tries to impersonate as a legitimate vehicle.
8. Resistance to Man-in-the-middle attack(8 ): The network must be able to detect, if there is any modification of the messages
while in the transmission by a malicious vehicle.
9. Resistance to Collusion attacks(9 ): The algorithm must be secure against collusion, ensuring that multiple vehicles cannot
collaborate to forge a valid aggregate signature.
10. Resistance to Type-I Adversary(1 ): The scheme must remain secure even when an adversary 1 is unable to obtain the
system’s master key but can replace or modify a vehicle’s public key.
11. Resistance to Type-II Adversary(2 ): The scheme must remain secure even if an adversary 2 gains access to the system’s
master key but cannot obtain any vehicle’s private key.
12. Resistance to Type-III Adversary(3 ): Type-III adversary resistance has two orthogonal goals listed below.
• Resistance to Message injection attacks: The algorithm must ensure that no adversary can inject unauthorized messages
into the aggregate signature without being detected.
• Resistance to Signature swapping attacks: The algorithm must be secure against signature swapping, ensuring that valid
signatures cannot be rearranged or replaced within the aggregate signature without detection.
4. Literature review
4.1. Overview of technology evaluation
To solve the privacy and security issues that come with vehicular ad hoc networks, a variety of authentication techniques have
been put forth. In traditional PKI, a Certificate Authority (CA) provides and maintains digital certificates for every vehicle, was
adopted by early VANETs authentication techniques [1,17,18]. However, this method increased communication overhead because
each message required a certificate to be transmitted, places a heavy management burden on the CA to handle millions of vehicle
certificates, including issuance, renewal and revocation operations. Which requires huge memory to store significant amount of data
and to maintain multiple certificates. The investigation of alternative cryptographic paradigms was spurred by these constraints.
To eliminate certificate management overhead, researchers investigated Identity-Based Public Key Cryptography (ID-PKC),
introduced by shamir [19]. In ID-PKC frameworks, user’s public keys are derived directly from publicly available information,
such as vehicle identification numbers or pseudonyms, thereby eliminating the need for certificates entirely. A trusted Private
Key Generator(PKG) computes private keys for users based on their identities using a master secret key. While several ID-PKCbased authentication schemes have been proposed for VANETs, demonstrating reduced communication overhead and simplified key
management, this approach suffers from an inherent key escrow problem. The PKG can compute any user’s private key, enabling
impersonation or decryption of messages. For safety-critical vehicular applications where message authenticity directly impacts
human lives, this centralized trust assumption is unacceptable. To simultaneously eliminate certificates and avoid key escrow, AIRiyami et al. [5] introduced CL-PKC in 2003, distributing trust between a KGC and users through a novel key generation protocol.
The KGC creates a partial private key based on each user’s identity and master secret, while users independently select secret values
unknown to the KGC. Since complete private keys requires both parts, neither users nor the KGC can forge signature on behalf of
others, making CL-PKC attractive for VANETs authentication by removing both certificate overhead and full key escrow. However,
conventional certificateless signature schemes verify signatures individually, causing computational delays when vehicles and RSUs
receive multiple simultaneous messages. To address this Boneh et al. [6] proposed aggregate signature in 2003, enabling multiple
signatures to be combined and verified collectively. Building on this, Castro and Dahab [20] merged certificateless cryptography
with aggregate signatures in 2007, introducing the first CLAS scheme-providing certificate free operation, reduced key escrow risks
and efficient batch verification suited for VANETs.

4.2. Foundational CLAS constructions
Castro and Dahab [20] proposed the first practical CLAS construction with formal security proofs, though they later identified
flaws in their security reductions. At the same time Gong et al. [21] introduced a pairing based CLAS scheme without formal
demonstrations, which was demonstrated to be susceptible to Type-I adversaries [22]. In 2008 Zhang et al. [23] proposed one more
CLAS construction which, however, was refined by shim [24] in 2011 and demonstrated that it required clock synchronization
also susceptible to coalition attacks. In 2009 Zhang and Zhang [25] re-defined the CLAS security model and developed a
more stringent analytical model. A practical bilinear-map construction was then followed by Gong et al. [26] in 2010, but at
linearly scaling overhead. Zhang et al. [27] during 2010–2011 introduced a scheme targeting many-to-one-authentication, but still
required synchronization for randomness aggregation. A significant milestone came from Xiong et al. [28], achieving constanttime verification with a fixed number of pairing operations regardless of aggregate signatures, without requiring synchronized
randomness. However, as detailed below, this and most other foundational constructions were subsequently found to critical security
vulnerabilities.
4.3. Recurring vulnerability cycle: Attack taxonomy
A defining characteristic of the CLAS literature is a persistent cycle of proposal, cryptanalysis, and attempted improvement. We
categorize these vulnerabilities by attack type to reveal systematic patterns.
4.3.1. Type-I and type-II adversary attacks
In standard CLAS security models, Type-I adversaries are external attackers who can replace public keys but lack the master
secret, while Type-II adversaries represent a malicious KGC possessing the master secret. He et al. [29] sowed in 2014 that Xiong
et al.’s [28] efficient scheme was insecure against Type-II adversaries. Tu et al. [30] independently demonstrated systematic TypeII forgery vectors and proposed improvements. Zhang et al. [31] contributed comprehensive analysis frameworks. Earlier Xiong
et al. [32,33] attempted to enhance security for mobile environments(2011–2012) also proved vulnerable under subsequent analysis.
Zhan and Wang [34] showed in 2019 that kumar et al.’s [35] health-care focused scheme similarly allowed for Type-II forgery.
4.3.2. Coalition attacks
Coalition attacks involve legitimate signers colluding to forge aggregate signatures on behalf of non-participants. Shim [24] first
identified this in Zhang et al.’s [23] scheme. Yang et al. [36] in 2018 proved that Cheng et al.’s [37] improved scheme is vulnerable,
demonstrating that coalition resistance requires explicit design considerations frequently overlooked in CLAS constructions.
4.3.3. Malicious-but-passive KGC attacks
A more subtle model of threat is that, the KGC deviating from key generation protocols in undetectable ways. Li et al. [38] showed
that the privacy preservation and batch verification scheme [39] of the VANETs and the scheme proposed by Horng et al. [40] failed
to resist such attacks in 2016. In 2018, Li, Yuan and Zhang [41] group showed that even a refined scheme by He et al. [29] was
still insecure, and they came up with a new construction using ECCDH.
4.3.4. Public key replacement and universal attacks
Shen et al. [42] developed a universal attack in 2016, proving adversaries could generate valid signatures without any user’s
secret information. Cahyadi et al.’s 2022 [43] CLAS construction was found vulnerable to public key replacement and malicious
KGC attacks, with Li et al. [44] in 2025 revealing the vulnerabilities and proposing improvements.

4.3.5. Fully Chosen-Key attacks
Wang et al. [45] introduced a paradigm-shifting threat model in 2019: fully chosen-key attacks, where adversaries possess all
signer’s private keys but produce invalid individual signatures that aggregate into valid composites. Their analysis revealed that no
existing pairing-based CLAS scheme secure under traditional models could withstand these attacks, fundamentally challenging the
security foundations of the entire class of pairing-based constructions.
4.3.6. Critical observations
The recurring vulnerability pattern reveals systematic issues: schemes built on similar bilinear pairing foundations share common
algebraic weakness; progressively sophisticated adversary models consistently outpace existing security guarantees and proposed
improvements frequently address specific attacks without achieving comprehensive security, continuing the break-and-fix cycle.
4.4. Application specific adaptations
A number of CLAS schemes have been constructed for specific domains. Horng et al. [40] focused on VANETs using privacy
preservation, batch verification; Kumar et al. [35] scaled CLAS to healthcare IoT; Xu et al. [46] considered secure routing in resource
constrained VANETs using the ECCDH assumption. More recently, Nyangaresi et al. [47] constructed more powerful authentication
protocols, Li et al. [48] suggested certificateless signcryption with non-repudiation property, and Zhang et al. [49] combined with
pairings with fuzzy extraction methods. Nevertheless, domain-specific properties could never pay off in overcoming underlying
cryptographic flaws: Li et al. [38] defeated Horng et al. [40], Zhan and Wang [34], Kumar et al. [35] and Cahayadi et al. [43]
defeated by Li et al. [44] shows that domain-specific adaptations are never able to replace fundamentally secure cryptographic
primitives.
4.5. The pairing computation bottleneck
In addition to security vulnerabilities, there is a common performance limitation of all pairing-based CLAS schemes. Bilinear
pairing operations are computationally intensive when compared to basic elliptic curve operations such as scalar multiplication and
point addition. Xu et al. [46] noted that most existing schemes suffer from either security vulnerabilities or excessive computational
costs. This overhead is especially prohibitive in resource constrained settings such as IoT devices, mobile systems and vehicular OBUs
with limited processing power and energy. Even Xiong et al.’s [28] constant-time verification scheme required pairing operations
whose absolute cost remained impractical for time-critical applications.
4.5.1. Summary and research gaps
Table 1 provides a comparative study of the pairing-based CLAS schemes, categorized by their known vulnerabilities, verification
cost and whether formal security proofs were provided. The table highlights the persistent pattern of the security failures across the
literature. The literature reveals three critical gaps: (i) pairing-based CLAS constructions continue to fall to increasingly sophisticated
attacks, with Wang et al. [45] proving no existing scheme is can withstand fully-chosen key attacks; (ii) bilinear pairing overhead
cannot be tolerated within real-time vehicular performance specifications; and (iii) application-specific adaptations are not able to
absorb underlying cryptographic weaknesses.
These gaps collectively motivate the pairing-free CLAS schemes that rely solely on ECC. By using scalar multiplication instead of
bilinear pairings achieve both provable security against comprehensive adversary models and computational efficiency suitable for
environments with limited resources, including VANETs, IoT systems, and mobile edge computing. For example, J Cui et al. [50]
proposed a pairing-free CLAS scheme tailored for VANETs, thereby obviating the necessity for bilinear pairings and map-to-point
hash functions. Gayathri et al. [51] put forward a pairing-free CLAS scheme for healthcare wireless medical sensor networks that
allows for anonymous authentication while greatly lowering the computational overhead. J Liu et al. [52] introduced an enhanced
pairing-free CLAS scheme that mitigates security vulnerabilities found in prior healthcare schemes while preserving computational
efficiency. Later, Thumbur et al. [53] created an efficient and most secure pairing-free CLAS-scheme for VANETs which is provably
secure based on ECCDH. Since then, many pairing-free CLAS schemes have been published in the literature, each meeting different
security needs and use cases. The literature survey section will provide a comprehensive review of these plans and compare them
with one another. But our review is restricted to ECC based CLAS schemes for vehicular ad-hoc networks.
The research direction was initiated for VANETs in 2018 through the work of J Cui et al. [50], who proposed the first
elliptic curve-based CLAS scheme that eliminates the computational overhead of bilinear pairings. These foundational contributions
opened new avenues for efficiency-oriented cryptographic design, prompting substantial research activity that has yielded numerous
improved and specialized ECC-based CLAS schemes. The subsequent evolution of this domain has been characterized by diverse
research objectives, including enhanced security models addressing sophisticated adversarial scenarios, optimization strategies
targeting resource-constrained environments, and domain-specific adaptations. Researchers have pursued multiple evolutionary
paths, with some focusing on cryptanalysis and security enhancements of existing schemes, while others have explored novel
construction methodologies and optimized implementations for specific deployment contexts. To provide a holistic understanding
of this rapidly evolving field, we present a comprehensive comparative study of existing schemes, examining their structural design
principles, systematically analyzing their security models, cryptographic assumptions, computational complexity, performance
characteristics, and practical applicability across different deployment scenarios. By concentrating on pairing-free constructions,
this review addresses the most promising direction for practical VANETs authentication, providing a comprehensive understanding
of current capabilities, limitations, and future research opportunities in this critical domain.

5.4. Why the attack succeeds
The following vulnerabilities in the system make the cancellation Attack feasible:
• Additive aggregation rule: The aggregation procedure is linear, which allows it to be easy to synthesize aggregate signatures and
prove them.
• Aggregate-only norm check: The global norm condition will not protect from tampering because the modifications cancel out
perfectly.
As a result, the opponent is able to arbitrarily manipulate selected signature elements and provide an aggregate signature that
is still considered valid.
5.5. Consequences
An adversary can use the cancellation attack to:
• Compromise or alter the personal contributions without detection and minimize or remove the influence of certain signers.
• Shrinkage of accountability through hiding the efforts of every vehicle and misguides the entire VANETs system, which depends
on the data that an aggregate signature verifies.
The adversary does not forge a signature; rather, it exploits the linearity of the aggregation mechanism.

Randomized Aggregation Coefficients: To aid in preventing the cancellation attack, append random aggregation coefficients of
each signature. In the aggregation, the RSU collects scalar sampled new random values 𝑟𝑖 ∈ Z𝑞 and accumulates them to obtain the

Despite significant advancements in ECC-based CLAS schemes for VANETs, several critical challenges remain unresolved. This
section identifies the current limitations and proposes promising research directions to address the evolving security and performance
requirements of next-generation vehicular networks.

7.1. Current challenges in existing methods
• Computational Overhead in Situations with High Densities: Although the CLAS schemes based on ECC are more efficient than
pairing-based methods, scalability is still an issue in crowded metropolitan settings. The aggregation and verification overhead
can surpass acceptable real-time restrictions (usually 100–300 ms for safety-critical applications) when hundreds of cars
broadcast safety messages simultaneously within the coverage area of a single RSU. The verification difficulty of existing
techniques increases linearly or almost linearly depending on the number of aggregated signatures, becoming unfeasible when
processing 𝑛 > 500 signatures at once.
• Efficiency and Security Trade-off : Current ECC-based CLAS schemes face a trade-off between efficiency and strong security.
Schemes designed to resist threats such as Type-II forgeries, public-key substitution, and key-replacement attacks often require
extra cryptographic checks during signing and verification. Incorporating secure key-validation steps, for instance, introduces
additional elliptic-curve multiplications and can raise the signing cost by roughly 30%–50% compared to less robust designs
• Danger of Malevolent Aggregation: The assumption of honest-but-curious aggregators is a basic drawback of several existing
CLAS methods. RSUs or intermediary vehicles carrying out aggregation may be compromised or act maliciously in real-world
VANETs deployments. Malevolent aggregators could:
– Use fake signatures to frame participants who are sincere.
– Reorder signatures to change the information regarding the message sequence.
– Aggregation delay that results in a safety-critical message timeout.
There is a single point of failure in the verification chain because the majority of current schemes lack the means to identify
or validate such aggregator misbehavior.
• Standardized security models are lacking : A single security model for assessing CLAS schemes on VANETs has not yet been agreed
upon by the research community. Direct comparison is challenging since different works use different adversarial models,
security definitions, and threat assumptions. While some techniques claim standard model security without thorough formal
analysis, others offer security proofs in the random oracle model. This disarray slows down real-world deployment and prevents
methodical evaluation.
• Limited Testing and Real-World Implementation: Although several ECC-based CLAS schemes provide good theoretical performance
metrics, there is still a lack of empirical validation using real VANETs hardware (OBUs and RSUs). Simulations frequently
ignore real-world factors such as:
– Processing delays in the network layer and protocol overhead.
– Cache behavior and memory limitations for embedded devices.
– Effects of running many processes at once (GPS processing, program execution).
– Effects of packet loss and wireless channel contention.
• Revocation’s Privacy-Efficiency Conundrum: A continuing difficulty is to provide efficient revocation while maintaining conditional privacy. The revocation of vehicle’s credentials should cause all previously issued signatures to become invalid or
traceable. However, there is a significant communication cost associated with updating the revocation lists of every participant
in a highly mobile network. Revocation strategies based on accumulators and bloom filters add complexity to verification while
lowering bandwidth needs. Existing methods do not strike the best possible balance among revocation granularity, privacy
protection, and verification effectiveness.
• The Danger of Quantum Computing : The advent of quantum computing presents a long-term existential threat, even as ECC
provides sufficient security against traditional adversaries. In polynomial time, Shor’s method can solve the ECDLP on quantum
computers with enough power. Given that automobiles last only ten to fifteen years, the cryptographic techniques used
today need to account for post-quantum dangers. CLAS systems must be fundamentally redesigned in order to switch to
quantum-resistant alternatives.
7.2. Future research directions
Future studies on ECC-based certificateless aggregate signature (CLAS) systems in VANETs could go in the following directions:
• Adaptive and Dynamic Aggregation: Vehicles regularly enter and exit communication ranges due to the dynamic topology
of VANETs. In order to add or remove signatures without re-aggregating all previously created ones, future ECC-based
CLAS schemes should include dynamic aggregation and disaggregation. This will lower the computational cost and increase
scalability during real-time message authentication.
• Effective Revocation Procedures: In current CLAS systems, revocation of harmful or inactive vehicles is still difficult. Ensuring that
compromised or expired keys are effectively excluded from aggregation through the integration of cryptographic accumulators
or lightweight blockchain-based revocation systems helps preserve overall security and trust.
• Adaptability to DoS Attacks: Mechanisms to identify and stop Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks against signature verification
procedures should be incorporated into future plans. To stop attackers from flooding RSUs with too many verification requests,
pre-verification filters, batch verification prioritization, and adaptive rate-limiting can be implemented.

• Improved Privacy with the Use of Pseudonyms: ECC-based CLAS schemes that utilize adaptive pseudonym management can further
enhance privacy. Unlinkable aggregation, in conjunction with context-aware and periodic pseudonym modifications, can
guard against tracking attacks while preserving authentication accuracy. Hybrid pseudonym creation methods and randomized
pseudonym intervals can improve anonymity even further.
• Effective Identification of Invalid Signatures: It can take a lot of effort to find flawed individual signatures when aggregate
verification is unsuccessful. Reliability in congested vehicle situations can be increased by creating effective invalid signature
search algorithms that localize the faulty signature or signatures with little verification overhead, utilizing hierarchical or
Merkle tree-based techniques.
• Secure CLAS Post-Quantum Designs: Future studies should investigate hybrid ECC–lattice-based CLAS designs because ECC is
susceptible to quantum attacks. By offering quantum resistance and maintaining the effectiveness of elliptic curves, such
techniques can guaranty the long-term security of VANETs authentication systems.
• Combining AI with Edge Technologies: Performance can be further improved by integrating edge computing and artificial
intelligence (AI) into ECC-based CLAS. While AI-driven models can forecast trust levels, identify irregularities, and optimize
verification workloads in real time, edge nodes can manage partial aggregation or pre-verification.
8. Conclusion
With a focus on pairing-free designs for effective V2V and V2I authentication, this paper surveys elliptic curve-based CLAS
techniques for VANETs. By eliminating certificate administration and key escrow problems while guaranteeing minimal computational and communication costs, ECC-based CLAS schemes successfully satisfy important security needs in VANETs. Achieving
constant-time verification for large signature aggregates is still a significant research challenge, despite considerable advancements.
To guarantee long-term security in intelligent transportation systems, future research should also concentrate on implementing
quantum-resistant primitives, strengthening resilience to denial-of-service assaults, and refining revocation mechanisms.
