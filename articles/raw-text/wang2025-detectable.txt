ABSTRACT
Vehicular Ad-hoc Networks (VANETs) have significantly improved the efficiency of traffic systems, but
there are many security concerns, such as reliable message exchange and privacy-preserving. Besides, under
resource-limited conditions, many signed safety-related messages need to be verified in a short period of
time. For such, many Certificate-Less Aggregate Signature (CLAS) schemes are proposed. However, some
existing CLAS schemes need an efficient algorithm to detect invalid signatures when aggregate verification
fails or the proposed algorithms have some unnecessary computation overhead. To overcome such issues, we
propose an efficient CLAS scheme that not only fulfills security requirements in VANETs but also provides an
improved algorithm to detect invalid signatures with the corresponding real identities. In addition, under the
Random Oracle Model (ROM) based Computational Diffie–Hellman (CDH) assumption, we demonstrate that
the proposed CLAS scheme is existentially unforgeable under adaptively chosen message attacks (EUF-ACMAs).
Performance analysis shows that the proposed scheme is more advantageous in terms of computation overhead
and security than other existing schemes.

1. Introduction
With the advances in vehicular and transportation technologies,
there is a substantial rise in the number of vehicles. While this has
made daily travel more convenient for humans, it has also exacerbated
numerous traffic-related issues, such as congestion and accidents. In
response to these challenges, VANETs emerge as time requires. A typical VANET system consists of four key components: Application Server
(AS), Trusted Authorities (TAs), Roadside Units (RSUs), and vehicles
equipped with On-Board Units (OBUs). Dedicated Short-Range Communication (DSRC), a wireless communication technology, facilitates data
transmission through two main channels: Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) and
Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I) communication. Subsequently, vehicles
primarily utilize DSRC to transmit safety-related messages, contributing
to enhanced road safety.
VANETs play a crucial role in monitoring real-time traffic flow
and predicting driving conditions, providing valuable assistance to
drivers when navigating various circumstances. Moreover, VANETs

enable vehicles to communicate with each other and exchange information about their speed, direction, and location. This information
can be used to detect potential collision risks and alert drivers to
take evasive actions. Beyond addressing traffic issues, VANETs can also
deliver entertainment content, news updates, and other information
to passengers, enhancing the overall driving experience. Nevertheless,
chances frequently provide difficulties as well. Due to the inherent
limitations of wireless communication, attackers can exploit these vulnerabilities to execute various attacks [1]. For example, by pretending
to be legitimate vehicles, attackers disseminate false safety-related
messages to disrupt the effectiveness of the traffic management system
and mislead drivers, which may further lead to inefficient traffic flow
and traffic congestion or accidents. Hence, it is essential to ensure
reliable message exchange in VANETs. Besides, the privacy-preserving
also needs to be considered [2–5]. In VANET, a vehicle’s privacy information includes current position, driving direction, and traffic tracking,
among others. If this information is transmitted in plain text, attackers
can deduce sensitive information about the vehicle once it is collected

Numerous PKI-based authentication schemes were proposed for
VANETs. Azees et al. [6] presented an anonymous authentication
scheme for VANETs and Zhang et al. [7] presented a cross-domain
authentication scheme for VANETs. In these schemes, the certificate
authority (CA) issues digital certificates to users who submit their
public key, ensuring that the user’s public key is legitimate. However,
these schemes suffer from some problems arising from certificate
management, which result in extra computation and communication
overhead.
Shamir et al. [8] introduced the Identity-based Public Key Cryptosystem (ID-PKC). Where in ID-PKC-based authentication schemes [9,
10], the private key is generated by the key generation center (KGC)
according to the user’s identity information, which functions as their
public key. In addition to resolving certificate management issues, this
also removes the necessity for digital certificates. In [11], Shim et al.
designed an authentication scheme suitable for V2I communication,
which does not need to preload the system master key into the tamperproof device of each vehicle, and also realizes the security requirements
such as message integrity and unlinkability. In order to further reduce
the computational overhead, Lo and Tsai [12] and Ali et al. [13]
proposed their schemes respectively, in which [12] is suitable for V2I
communication, and [13] is suitable for V2V communication. However,
since the KGC is the entity that issues all users’ private keys, key escrow
has a problem because it allows the KGC to forge any user’s signature
and substitute any user for signature verification in these schemes.
Al-Riyami and Paterson [14] introduced the Certificate-Less Public
Key Cryptosystem (CL-PKC). By solving the key escrow problem while
maintaining the benefits of ID-PKC, the user’s private key consists of
a secret value selected by the user and a partial private key generated
by KGC in CL-PKC-based authentication schemes [15]. Yeh et al. [16]
proposed a certificate-less signature scheme to ensure that smart devices in the Internet of Things (IoT) can interact securely. However, Jia
et al. [17] pointed out that any malicious attacker could impersonate
the KGC and forge partial private for legitimate users and that their
scheme was not resistant to Type-𝐼 attackers in [16]. Jia et al. proposed
a new signature scheme to address these flaws; however, this scheme
was not resistant to Type-𝐼 attackers. Not only that, Xiang et al. [18]
also found that [17] was not resistant to Type-𝐼 𝐼 attackers. By modifying the signature phase, verification phase in [17], Xiang et al. designed
an improved authentication scheme in [18]. Recently, [18] was not
resistant to Type-𝐼 attackers, as Ma et al. [19] pointed out.
In addition to reliable message exchange and privacy protection,
according to the DSRC protocol, vehicles in VANETs broadcast numerous safety-related messages every 100–300 ms [20]. However, when
multiple users are involved in signature verification, the verifiers in the
certificate-less signature scheme need to transmit and verify each signature one by one, which leads to a large amount of network bandwidth
being occupied and also increases the burden of computational cost. In
contrast, the CLAS not only inherits the security and privacy advantages
of the CL-PKC, but also further improves the verification efficiency and
significantly reduces the computation cost, such as [21–33].

CLAS is appropriate for VANET environments because of its efficiency and security. Nevertheless, some of the existing CLAS schemes
have serious security issues and are unable to defend against various threats from different adversaries. For V2I communication, Cui
et al. [21] claimed that their scheme could resist both types of attackers
under the ROM. Nevertheless, Kamil and Ogundoyin [22] pointed out
that [21] could not withstand Type-𝐼 𝐼 attackers, and an improved
CLAS scheme was proposed. Han et al. [23] highlighted that the
scheme [22] was not resilient to both types of attackers and proposed a
CLAS scheme to safeguard the data’s authenticity and integrity, demonstrating that their proposed scheme could withstand both types of
attackers. Unfortunately, because [23] did not ensure the system master
key’s secrecy, their scheme remained insecure. In addition, Zhong
et al. [24] presented an efficient CLAS scheme for VANETs and showed
it was resistant to both types of attackers. Mei et al. [25] showed that
the scheme [24] was not resilient to Type-𝐼 𝐼 attackers and presented
a privacy-preserving CLAS scheme that uses full aggregation to reduce
bandwidth and computation cost. Nevertheless, this scheme [25] was
not resilient to forgery attacks, as Yu et al. [26] pointed out. In [29],
Liu et al. presented a CLAS for IoT and depicted that their scheme
is EUF-ACMAs under the ROM, dramatically reducing the time to
validate big data from IoT terminals. However, it is unfortunate that
the scheme [29] is not resilient to Type-𝐼 𝐼 attackers. Considering
the limited bandwidth in VANETs and to reduce the computational
overhead, Kumar et al. [28] constructed a new CLAS. They claimed that
the scheme was secure under the ROM and could preserve conditional
privacy, requiring less overhead in computation and communication
than some schemes. In 2010, Zhang et al. [27] proposed an efficient
many-to-one authentication with CLAS. Based on this foundation, Yang
et al. [30] applied the scheme to the safety warning system in fogcloud-based VANETs. In [31], Kar et al. proposed a new CLAS for
wireless sensor networks but The pairing operation required grew linearly with the number of signers. Recently, based ECC, Zhou et al. [32]
and Zhu et al. [33] proposed their own CLAS for VANETs.
On the other hand, when aggregate verification fails in a CLAS
scheme, the verifier needs to detect invalid signatures with the corresponding real identities, providing a basis for subsequent accountability
in traffic accident investigations. To solve this problem, binary tree
detection algorithms [20,23,36] have been proposed. Unfortunately,
these schemes suffered from some unnecessary aggregate verification
operations, which led to unnecessary computational overhead.
1.2. Motivations and contributions
Through the research and summary of the above CLAS schemes, it
can be found that CLAS schemes have the following deficiencies:
(1) Some schemes are based on the assumption that a tamper-proof
device is impenetrable, but this assumption is too perfect. In fact, an
attacker can obtain sensitive data, including the master key of the
system, by launching a side channel attack [20].
(2) Some schemes cannot meet the security requirements of the actual application environment, such as public-key replacement attacks,
signature forgery attacks, etc.
(3) When aggregate verification fails, most schemes do not provide
an algorithm to detect invalid signatures from all received signatures,
and some invalid signature detection algorithms have some unnecessary verification steps, resulting in redundant computation overhead.
In this work, we propose an efficient CLAS scheme to handle these
issues, and the contributions are as follows:
(1) The proposed scheme not only achieves the security, conditional
privacy-reserving, and efficiency required by VANETs, but also provides
an improved invalid signature detection algorithm.
(2) Under the ROM based on the CDH assumption, the proposed
scheme is EUF-ACMAs proven, also satisfying several security requirements in VANETs.
(3) Performance analysis shows that the proposed scheme is more
appropriate for VANETs with stronger security and lower computation
overhead.

1.1. Related work
Boneh et al. [34] presented the concept of aggregate signatures,
which provides an algorithm for combining multiple signatures from
different users into one short signature and another algorithm to verify
the short signature. Combining this concept and the benefits of CLPKC, Gonge et al. [35] first proposed two CLAS schemes and provided
a CLAS security model. However, their security model has several
shortcomings.

2.4. System model
Fig. 1 illustrates that VANETs consist of two layers. The AS and
the TAs make up the upper layer. The AS can assist in gathering
and analyzing traffic data as well as authenticating the safety-related
messages received from the RSUs. The TAs consist of two modules:
the KGC and the TRA. When a new vehicle is added to the system,
the KGC generates a partial private key(𝑑𝑖 ) for it to encrypt and
decrypt sensitive information during communication, and the TRA is
responsible for assigning pseudonym identity(𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 ) for it to protect
their privacy. In cases of traffic violations, accidents, disputes, etc.,
the TRA can quickly and accurately track the real identity (𝐼 𝐷𝑖 ) of
malicious vehicles through 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 . The RSUs and vehicles make up the
bottom layer. As a key node for information exchange between vehicles
and the AS, RSUs are widely distributed on both sides of the road. They
are not only responsible for collecting and real-time publishing various
traffic information, such as road condition updates, accident warnings,
etc., but also support information exchange and promote collaborative
driving between vehicles. Every vehicle has an OBU that integrates
multiple sensors and communication technologies, which can real-time
obtain key information such as the vehicle’s position, speed, direction,
etc., and communicate wirelessly with other vehicles and RSUs through
DSRC.

2.5. Security model
By comparing to adversaries in [37], the proposed scheme also
considers the following two types of attackers:
Type-𝐼: This adversary 𝐴1 imitates a malevolent vehicle that can
replace any user’s public key but cannot obtain the KGC’s master secret
key.
Type-𝐼 𝐼: This adversary 𝐴2 imitates a malevolent KGC that can obtain
the KGC’s master secret key but cannot replace vehicle’s public key.

3. The proposed scheme
We present the proposed CLAS scheme in detail next, which stages
are as follows:
∙Setup: In this phase, the TAs and the AS generate params. The procedure in detail is as follows:
(1) After inputting security parameters l, the TAs generate two groups
with the same prime order of q: 𝐺1 and 𝐺2 , which can be used to build
a bilinear map 𝑒 ∶ 𝐺1 × 𝐺1 → 𝐺2 . Furthermore, 𝐺1 has P as a generator.
(2) The TRA selects 𝑡 ∈ 𝑍𝑞∗ randomly and computes 𝑇𝑝𝑢𝑏 = 𝑡𝑃 ;
(3) The KGC selects 𝑘 ∈ 𝑍𝑞∗ randomly and computes 𝐾𝑝𝑢𝑏 = 𝑘𝑃 ;
(4) The AS selects 𝑦 ∈ 𝑍𝑞∗ randomly and computes 𝑌𝑝𝑢𝑏 = 𝑦𝑃 ;
(5) The TAs choose seven hash functions: 𝐻0,4,5,6 → 𝑍𝑞∗ , 𝐻1,2,3 → 𝐺1 ,
and output params = (𝐺1 , 𝐺2 ,e,q,P, 𝐾𝑝𝑢𝑏 , 𝑇𝑝𝑢𝑏 , 𝑌𝑝𝑢𝑏 , 𝐻0,1,2,3,4,5,6 ).
∙Pseudonym Identity Generation: In this phase, the TRA generates
𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 for 𝑉𝑖 . The procedure in detail is as follows:
(1) The TRA chooses 𝑢𝑖 ∈ 𝑍𝑞∗ randomly and calculates
𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,1 = 𝐻0 (𝑡) ⊕ 𝑢𝑖 , 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,2 = 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 ⊕ 𝐻0 (𝑢𝑖 ∥ 𝑇 𝑃𝑖 )
where the pseudonym identity’s validity term is represented by 𝑇 𝑃𝑖 ;
(2) The TRA transmits 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 = (𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,1 , 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,2 , 𝑇 𝑃𝑖 ) to 𝑉𝑖 .
∙Partial Private Key Generation: In this phase, the KGC generates 𝑑𝑖
for 𝑉𝑖 . The procedure in detail is as follows:

4.2. Security analysis
The proposed CLAS scheme fulfills the subsequent security features
and unforgeability, and some additional analysis follows.
(1) Message authentication: In our scheme, every message must be
signed before being forwarded to the RSU. The AS will then confirm
that the message is authentic. Furthermore, our CLAS scheme is EUFACMAs. Message authentication will thereafter be supported by the
CLAS scheme.
(2) Conditional anonymity: In our scheme, all vehicles utilize
pseudonym identity 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 = (𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,1 , 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,2 , 𝑇 𝑃𝑖 ) for communication
instead of real identity 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 . Moreover, the real identity 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 may be
extracted by the TRA based on 𝑢𝑖 = 𝐻0 (𝑡)⊕ 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,1 , 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 = 𝐻0 (𝑢𝑖 ∥ 𝑇 𝑃𝑖 )⊕
𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,2 if necessary. As a result, conditional anonymity is supported by
our scheme.
(3) Unlinkability: Vehicles sign various messages in communication
using distinct pseudonym identities and matching private keys. The previous and current pseudonym identities are unrelated to one another,
so unlinkability is supported by our scheme.
4) Traceability: If someone needs to be held responsible for an
accident, the TRA can retrieve their real identity 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 according to
𝑢𝑖 = 𝐻0 (𝑡) ⊕ 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,1 , 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 = 𝐻0 (𝑢𝑖 ∥ 𝑇 𝑃𝑖 ) ⊕ 𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖,2 , thus traceability
is supported by our scheme.
(5) Replay attack: To guarantee that the authentication message is
fresh, the timestamp 𝑇𝑖 is added to (𝑃 𝐼 𝐷𝑖 , 𝑚𝑖 , 𝑋𝑖 , 𝑇𝑖 , 𝜎𝑖 ), allowing the
RSU to detect replay attacks. As a result, our scheme can be resilient
to replay attacks.
(6) Impersonation attack: We can conclude from the security proof
that no PPT adversary can successfully fabricate a valid signature if
the CDH problem remains challenging. As a result, our scheme can be
resilient to impersonation attacks.
(7) Man-in-the-middle attack: Vehicles and RSUs verify one another’s identities. An attacker cannot fake a legitimate signature or pose
as a legitimate user, our scheme can be resilient to man-in-the-middle
attacks thereafter.

In this article, we introduce an efficient CLAS scheme that not only
meets the diverse security requirements of VANETs but also provides
an improved invalid signature detection algorithm. Furthermore, we
demonstrate that our CLAS scheme is EUF-ACMAs under the ROM.
Through performance analysis, our proposed scheme provides more
benefits in terms of security and computation cost, showing that it
is well-suited for VANETs. In future directions, we aim to extend
the applicability of our approach to the e-healthcare system, where
the verification of extensive medical data from medical terminals is
paramount. Additionally, to further reduce the computation overhead,
we will further work on the CLAS scheme without pairings and optimize
the invalid signature detection algorithm.
